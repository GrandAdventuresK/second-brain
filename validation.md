# Validation

Without a committee, the work is validated by markets and readers. For those to be *load-bearing* rather than flattering, two disciplines must hold. This file captures both.

## Why this file exists

Self-directed graduate study fails almost always for the same reason: there's no one with the standing to say "this isn't there yet." Markets and readers can fill that role — but only if the work is structured so they can actually disagree with it.

The two disciplines below are non-negotiable. Without them, the dissertation devolves into thoughtful self-flattery.

## Discipline 1 — Predict-before-acting at Grand Adventures

Every meaningful organizational intervention at the company is preceded by a written prediction:

- **What I'm changing.** Concretely.
- **Why I expect it to work.** The theory or mechanism, with a citation to the version of the framework it's testing.
- **What I expect to see, by when.** Specific, measurable, dated.
- **What would falsify the theory.** What outcome would tell me the model is wrong — not just that execution was off.

The prediction is logged *before* the intervention. Three to twelve months later (depending on the intervention), the actual outcome is recorded against the prediction. If the outcome diverges from prediction, the theory updates — not just the execution.

This is what makes the company a test of *thinking*, not just a test of *execution*.

Predictions live in `learning/leadership-and-identity/practice/predictions/<date>-<intervention>.md`. Each file:

1. Intervention name + date logged
2. Theory / mechanism (with a citation to the framework version it's testing)
3. Specific predicted outcomes, with dates
4. Falsification conditions
5. Actual outcomes (filled in when they arrive)
6. What the result tells me about the theory

## Discipline 2 — Falsifiable claims + critic-seeking in public writing

Every comp paper or public-facing piece:

- **Makes at least one claim that can be wrong.** Not a survey, not a meditation. An argument with a falsifiable spine.
- **Names what would falsify the claim.** Explicitly in the piece, or in private notes alongside it.
- **Is shown to two or three smart critics before publication.** People who will tell you what's weak, not what's good.
- **Tracks the reception** — who agreed, who disagreed, what arguments came back, what would update your view.

Critics aren't friends. Friends won't bite. Critics are people who have read enough of the field to push back on the substance, are willing to be uncomfortable, and have been explicitly asked to find the weak spot.

Reception is tracked at `learning/leadership-and-identity/practice/reception/<date>-<piece>.md`:

1. Title + publication venue + date
2. The falsifiable claim(s)
3. Critics named, what they said
4. Public reception (agreement, disagreement, traction)
5. What the response updates in your view

## What these disciplines together replace

A committee asks: *Have you read what's been done? Is your method defensible? Is your claim true? What if you're wrong?*

The two disciplines force you to ask the same questions of yourself, in writing, with stakes:

- Predict-before-act asks: *Is your theory true?*
- Falsifiable-claim asks: *Is your argument true?*
- Critic-seeking asks: *Have you found the weak spot before someone else does?*

Skip either discipline and the validators stop bidding the work upward.

## When to revisit

If you find yourself avoiding the disciplines — quietly skipping a prediction, softening a claim, picking gentle readers — that's the signal. Either the disciplines need rework, or you're flinching from the rigor you said you wanted.
